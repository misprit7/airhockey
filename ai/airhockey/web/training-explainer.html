01 / Architecture

A larger network with physical history.

The actor is a feed-forward network with 16 physical frames, spanning approximately 300 ms between oldest and newest samples. The history encoding uses the current frame and differences from earlier frames. The current three-way shot request is appended once. There is no recurrent hidden state.

02 / Private shot requests

The defender is not told the shot.

Each player receives its own request: left bank, right bank or straight, in its local view of the table. It cannot read the other player’s request. Both can see physical motion, so a defender can learn to infer a likely shot from a setup.

Blue actor’s private input

Red actor’s private input

Change blue’s request: red’s input does not change. This illustrates observation wiring, not a simulated rally. One-hot order is [left bank, right bank, straight].

One subtlety: a player retains its own last request when the puck leaves its half. That request is not information about the opponent. A training consistency loss compares defensive actions under different values of this own, expired request and discourages unnecessary differences.

Normally a fresh request is drawn when the puck enters a player’s half. Training also includes opponents with a fixed shot style for an entire game, so recognizing tendencies can help; their request is still not broadcast.

03 / Arrival actions

Specify where and how to arrive.

Every 20 ms the actor outputs a target position, terminal velocity, arrival time and acceleration allowance. A deterministic decoder predicts queued-command latency, constructs a reference motion, and uses the firmware motion model and guard to produce a feasible command. It does not choose a shot route or interception strategy.

04 / Optimization

Experience, score, update, repeat.

Collect simulated experience.

512 environments across four workers run 128 steps per rollout: 65,536 transitions, or 2.56 simulated seconds per environment.

Estimate which choices helped.

The independent critic predicts return. Generalized advantage estimation combines those predictions with rewards; gamma is 0.999 and GAE lambda is 0.98.

Update the actor and critic with PPO.

Two optimization epochs, minibatches of 2,048, actor learning rate 0.000008 and critic learning rate 0.0005. A KL target of 0.02 stops excessive minibatch updates. A small entropy term encourages exploration.

Retain skills and evaluate separately.

Training also uses reference-policy imitation, successful trajectory examples and defensive request consistency. Saved actors are evaluated without training noise, across requests, starts, opponents and thermal conditions.

Successful examples come from earlier learned actors and offline trajectory search. They teach the network through training losses; deployment does not call those teachers or replay a scripted shot. 05 / Practice and opponents

Full games plus focused practice.

The selected run is late-curriculum stage 5: 25% full games, with the remaining environments divided among stationary setup, receiving/recovery and defense. Practice isolates difficult situations often enough to learn them, while games test the whole sequence.

Receiving and recovery

Incoming drills use 3–8 m/s pucks. Seventy percent of receiving setups instead practice slow outgoing recovery at 0.15–1.2 m/s. The agent must get around the puck, control it, and convert possession into a shot—or make a useful immediate return.

Defense

Wide trajectories, randomized starting positions and opponent windups teach preparation as well as the final block. Warm motor starts force some practice under limited thermal headroom.

Self-play

The opponent pool holds six neural snapshots, supplemented by fixed neural references from earlier attack, defense and v2 training. It is refreshed periodically. Some practice also uses a moving neural opponent.

Reset rules matter

Capturing the puck does not immediately reset a receiving drill: follow-through allows conversion, loss of possession or a six-second limit. Full games use continuous rallies and 900-second truncations with value bootstrapping.

Reachable, quiet pucks stay in play. Only genuinely unreachable dead pucks are relaunched after eight seconds. This avoids hiding failed recoveries with a convenient reset, but exposes a real v3 weakness: some reachable edge positions still stall indefinitely.

07 / Physical constraints

Fast bursts have a sustained cost.

The policy’s configured limits are 12 m/s speed and 60 m/s² acceleration. The decoder and motion guard enforce instantaneous constraints. Separately, the actor observes fast/slow load memory for all four motors, and training penalizes sustained demand.

The provisional thermal model estimates load from directional acceleration, velocity and holding demands. It retains heat across ordinary goal resets. Training includes cold and correlated warm starts, a thermal gain of 1.3, and a failure threshold of 0.95. Live operation can use cached motor telemetry to inform the load estimate; modeled load is not identical to measured RMS.

1.0 represents the model’s overload level. The near-limit cost starts above 0.8.

Five sustained suites, each eight games of 900 seconds, recorded no modeled overload or firmware acceleration-cap violations. Peak normalized load reached approximately 0.975. Extended reachable-puck stalls occurred in these games; low motor load during inactivity is not evidence of good play.

Known replay failure: the pinned v3 clip can leave the puck near the left edge from roughly 20 seconds through the end of its 180-second window. Reachable contact exists. This is a policy recovery failure, not a reason to call the clip representative of consistently good play. These results do not justify a claim of superhuman defense.
09 / What changed

Keep the useful policy; close the experiment.

Neural restart

The scripted tactical hybrid was rejected. A single actor learned through curriculum, rewards, self-play and training-only examples.

Request conditioning and history

Private three-way shot requests restored shot variety. Physical history, a larger actor and preparation/recovery training followed.
